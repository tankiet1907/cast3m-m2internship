\section{Model calibration} \label{sec:calibration}

The accuracy of TH/THC model relies on a large number of material parameters -- intrinsic permeability, thermal conductivity and heat capacity, sorption properties, surface exchange coefficients and a complex set of constitutive laws describing their evolution with temperature and dehydration.

Many critical properties at high-temperature such as the evolution of intrinsic permeability, sorption isotherms are extremely difficult or even impossible to measure directly through experimental techniques and the values often contain uncertainties due to the intrusive nature of the sensors. Furthermore these are not measured directly in the Kalifa campaign.

A pre-determined parameter set therefore cannot be expected to reproduce the experiment. Consequently, a calibration procedure for the numerical models is strictly required to identify, within physically admissible ranges, the parameter set that makes the model reproduce the measured temperature and gas-pressure histories. Only once this reference case is calibrated and validated can the model be trusted for uncertainty studies in \cref{chapter5}.

\subsection{Calibration strategy} \label{sec:cal-strategy}
At the first approximation, the thermal problem is weakly coupled to the hydric one: the temperature field drives evaporation, dehydration and the gas-pressure build-up, while the effect of the hydric state on the temperature field is relatively small within the experimental range. This hierarchy promotes a two-stage calibration process:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Stage~1 -- Temperature.} The temperature evolution is calibrated independently of the pressure field. In this stage, the \emph{surface} (boundary) parameters should be calibrated first, then the \emph{bulk} conduction parameters.
  \item \textbf{Stage~2 -- Gas pressure.} With the reliably calibrated temperature field as a fixed thermal loading, the hydric parameters governing the gas-pressure evolution are adjusted, from the most influential to the secondary ones.
\end{enumerate}

The calibration targets are the temperature and gas-pressure histories measured at $10$, $20$, $30$, $40$ and $50~\mathrm{mm}$ from the heated face, together with the near-surface temperature recorded at $2~\mathrm{mm}$ (denoted the $0~\mathrm{mm}$ measurement).

\subsection{Automated parameter sweep} \label{sec:cal-automation}
The sweeps are driven by a Python script that launches Cast3M repeatedly, one run per parameter combination, without manual intervention. For each stage the parameter values to be tried are listed explicitly, and \texttt{itertools.product} builds every possible combination of those lists; a single loop then feeds each combination to Cast3M in turn.

This automation relies on two supporting pieces of code. 
\begin{itemize}
 \item First, the Cast3M input code itself exports the results of each run to a \texttt{.csv} file -- the temperature and gas-pressure histories at the measurement points.
 \item Second, a separate Python post-processing script reads these \texttt{.csv} files and plots the computed histories against the experimental measurements \cite{kalifa2000} and also against the results from Dorjan Dauti's thesis \cite{dauti2018}. All combinations can then be visually compared, and the best-fit set is selected by the author based on this comparison.
\end{itemize}
 
For example, in the gas-pressure calibration stage, the two parameters swept are the intrinsic permeability $K_0$ (denoted \texttt{KINT}) and the permeability--hydration coefficient $A_\Gamma$ (denoted \texttt{AK}), with the trial values:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $K_0 \in \{1\!\times\!10^{-19},\ 2\!\times\!10^{-19},\ 1\!\times\!10^{-20},\
  5\!\times\!10^{-20},\ 7.5\!\times\!10^{-20},\ 8.5\!\times\!10^{-20}\}~\mathrm{m^2}$
  ($6$ values);
  \item $A_\Gamma \in \{3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7,\ 8\}$ ($6$ values).
\end{itemize}
These two lists give $6\times 6 = 36$ parameter pairs, i.e.\ $36$ Cast3M runs. The same driver is reused by simply changing the value lists.

In every stage, a wider range of each parameter is first explored to bracket the admissible values; the search is then narrowed to the few representative values reported in the following sections, which are the ones shown in the figures.

\section{Temperature calibration} \label{sec:cal-temp}
    
\subsection{Surface temperature (00mm): boundary heat exchange coefficient} \label{sec:cal-surf}

The first quantity calibrated is the temperature of the heated face. In the experiment, heat is supplied by a radiant heater on the top-hot face, so the surface energy balance is mostly influenced by radiation and convection. As defined in the boundary conditions (\cref{eq:heated-face} of \cref{chapter3}), the total heat flux $q_{\mathrm{hot}}$ depends directly on two boundary coefficients: the convective heat-transfer coefficient $h^{\mathrm{hot}}$ and the surface emissivity $\varepsilon$.

These two parameters ($h^{\mathrm{hot}}$ and $\varepsilon$) are adjusted so that the computed $0~\mathrm{mm}$ history matches the measured near-surface ($2~\mathrm{mm}$) response. At this point the conduction parameters are held at their reference values; only the energy input at the surface is tuned.

The surface response is calibrated over three values of $\varepsilon$ ($0.65$, $0.68$, $0.72$) and three of $h_c$ ($9$, $10$, $11~\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$). 

Because the resulting curves differ only slightly, the full $3\times 3$ grid is not informative; instead the effect of each parameter is shown one at a time, varying it while the other is held at its retained value (\cref{fig:cal-surf-eps,fig:cal-surf-h}). The pair retained at this stage is $\varepsilon = 0.68$ and $h^{\mathrm{hot}} = 11~\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$.

Two distinct effects are concluded from \cref{fig:cal-surf-eps,fig:cal-surf-h}: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item The emissivity $\varepsilon$ is the most sensitive parameter (\cref{fig:cal-surf-eps}): because the radiative term acts in a strong non-linear way as $T_s^4$ (\cref{eq:heated-face}), a small increase in $\varepsilon$ raises the surface temperature substantially, and mostly in the high-temperature part of the curve, where radiation dominates the heat input.

  \item The convective coefficient $h^{\mathrm{hot}}$ acts more gently and mainly on the \emph{early} part of the curve (\cref{fig:cal-surf-h}): increasing $h^{\mathrm{hot}}$ raises the initial heating rate (the linear $h^{\mathrm{hot}}(T_\infty - T_s)$ term is largest while $T_s$ is still low), with only a slight additional rise in the tail. 
\end{itemize}

In short, $\varepsilon$ sets the level of the high-temperature plateau while $h^{\mathrm{hot}}$ controls the initial ramp; tuning the two together reproduces the measured surface temperature evolution.
 
\subsection{In-depth temperature: thermal conductivity and heat capacity} \label{sec:cal-depth}
 
Once the surface temperature is reproduced, the temperature rise at depth ($10$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$) is governed by heat conduction through the material, i.e.\ by the volumetric heat capacity $\rho C_p$ (\cref{eq:rhoCp}) and the thermal conductivity $\lambda$ (\cref{eq:lambda} and \cref{eq:lambda_d}), both given in \cref{appendixA}. Most of the quantities in these laws ($\phi$, $S_l$, the phase densities $\rho$ and the temperature $T$) are imposed by the state of the material. The two genuinely adjustable inputs are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the solid specific heat $C_{ps}$ (denoted \texttt{CS} in the model), which enters the heat capacity through Eq.~\ref{eq:rhoCp}. Although a temperature dependence is defined in Appendix~\ref{appendixA}, in the present implementation it is kept \emph{constant} -- it is not updated with temperature;
  \item the reference dry conductivity $\lambda_{d0}$ (denoted \texttt{KS} in the model), which scales the whole conductivity law.
\end{itemize}
 
A subtlety arises here: adjusting the surface condition in \cref{sec:cal-surf} changes the energy that actually enters the specimen, which in turn shifts the in-depth histories. Consequently $\lambda_{d0}$ and $C_{ps}$ cannot be calibrated in isolation -- they must be \emph{re-calibrated} jointly with the surface condition. 

The calibration therefore proceeds as a short journey, starting from the parameter set carried over from the surface stage and adjusting one quantity at a time.

\paragraph{Starting point.}
The run is first launched with the reference set inherited from the surface calibration in \cref{sec:cal-surf}: $\varepsilon = 0.68$, $h^{\mathrm{hot}} = 11~\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$, $C_{ps} = 948~\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}$ and $\lambda_{d0} = 1.67~\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}$.
Compared with the experiment and with the earlier results of Dauti (\cref{fig:cal-depth-ref}), the temperature is already well reproduced near the surface, but the deeper probes ($30$, $40$ and $50~\mathrm{mm}$) are over-predicted: the in-depth temperatures are too high and still need to be calibrated.
 
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/chapter4/Temp_compare_epsilon_0.68_h-hot_11}
  \caption{Temperature with the reference set ($\varepsilon=0.68$, $h_c=11$, $C_{ps}=948$, $\lambda_{d0}=1.67$), compared with the experiment and with Dauti's results.}
  \label{fig:cal-depth-ref}
\end{figure}
 
\paragraph{Step 1 -- reducing the conductivity $\lambda_{d0}$ (\texttt{KS}).} The conductivity acts mostly on the layers \emph{closer to the heated face}. To bring down the over-predicted $10$ and $20~\mathrm{mm}$ curves, $\lambda_{d0}$ is reduced (\cref{fig:cal-depth-ks}). This indeed lowers those curves, but it has a side effect: by slowing the inward transfer, heat accumulates near the surface, so the surface temperature becomes too high in the tail of the curve and, for the lowest values, the computation eventually crashes.

\paragraph{Step 2 -- re-calibrating the emissivity $\varepsilon$.} The surface overheating introduced by the lower $\lambda_{d0}$ is corrected by re-calibrating the emissivity. Reducing $\varepsilon$ lowers the radiative input at the surface (\cref{fig:cal-depth-eps}) and restores the $0~\mathrm{mm}$ response, stabilising the run.

\paragraph{Step 3 -- increasing the heat capacity $C_{ps}$ (\texttt{CS}).} The heat capacity governs the thermal inertia and is felt mostly at the \emph{deepest points}. To bring down the still over-predicted $30$, $40$ and $50~\mathrm{mm}$ curves, $C_{ps}$ is increased (\cref{fig:cal-depth-cs}): a larger $C_{ps}$ stores more energy per unit temperature rise, so the same heat flux produces a smaller, more delayed temperature increase in the core, lowering the internal temperatures.

\paragraph{Balancing $C_{ps}$ and $\lambda_{d0}$.} Increasing $C_{ps}$, however, cannot be pushed independently of $\lambda_{d0}$: the two parameters are coupled and must be balanced against each other. If $C_{ps}$ is raised while $\lambda_{d0}$ is kept low, the material both stores a large amount of energy and conducts it inward too slowly, so the incoming heat cannot diffuse away from the surface. This was observed directly for the combination $\varepsilon = 0.68$, $h^{\mathrm{hot}} = 11$, $C_{ps} = 960$, $\lambda_{d0} = 1.38$: the surface heat could not pass inward, the surface temperature rose abnormally, and the run crashed. The combination only becomes stable once the surface input is reduced -- by lowering the emissivity to $\varepsilon = 0.6$ (Step~2) -- so that a high $C_{ps}$ and a low $\lambda_{d0}$ can coexist. The calibration therefore consists of finding a balanced $(\lambda_{d0}, C_{ps})$ pair, together with the matching surface input, rather than tuning either conduction parameter on its own.

At the end of this journey the temperature is reproduced consistently at all depths ($0$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$). The final comparison against the Dauti and experimental curves is shown in \cref{fig:cal-depth-final}, providing a reliable thermal loading for the gas-pressure calibration.

\section{Gas-pressure calibration} \label{sec:cal-pressure}

With the temperature field fixed, the calibration of the gas-pressure evolution is addressed. The pressure build-up is governed primarily by three factors: the amount of water available for vaporisation (the internal source), how easily the vapour travels through the porous network (the intrinsic permeability $K$), and how easily it escapes from the heated surface into the environment (the convective mass-transfer coefficient $h_g$).
 
\subsection{Primary parameters: \texorpdfstring{$K_0$}{K0} and \texorpdfstring{$A_\Gamma$}{A_Gamma}} \label{sec:cal-K0-AK}
 
The intrinsic permeability $K_0$ (denoted \texttt{KINT}) and the permeability--hydration coefficient $A_\Gamma$ (denoted \texttt{AK}) are the two most influential parameters dictating the magnitude and acceleration of the gas transport. In the newly implemented THC model, the evolution of permeability is highly sensitive to the degradation of the effective hydration degree $\tilde{\Gamma}$ \cref{eq:ch3-perm}. 
 
These two parameters must be strictly balanced. If the combination makes $K$ too low, vapour cannot escape and the trapped vapor generates extremely severe pressure gradients; if it makes $K$ too high, the vapor escapes too easily into the environment, resulting in flattened, underestimated pressure profiles. In both cases, the Newton-Raphson solver diverges (crashes), so $K_0$ and $A_\Gamma$ have to be calibrated carefully together. 
 
\paragraph{Starting point -- $K_0 = 2\times10^{-19}~\mathrm{m^2}$.} The first sweep is performed at the initial reference permeability $K_0 = 2\times10^{-19}~\mathrm{m^2}$, varying $A_\Gamma$ over $3$, $4$ and $5$ (with $A_\Gamma = 3$ as the initial reference); the result is shown in \cref{fig:cal-pg-kint2e19}. Increasing $A_\Gamma$ raises the peaks, but this $K_0$ value is not physically representative of HPC, and the curves still do not fit the experimental pressures.

\paragraph{Reducing the permeability -- $K_0 = 8.5\times10^{-20}~\mathrm{m^2}$.} The permeability is therefore reduced to $K_0 = 8.5\times10^{-20}~\mathrm{m^2}$, which is more consistent with a dense HPC, and the $A_\Gamma$ sweep is repeated (\cref{fig:cal-pg-kint85e20}) to assess the effect of lowering $K_0$. Trapping the gas more effectively raises the peaks, and the pair $K_0 = 8.5\times10^{-20}~\mathrm{m^2}$, $A_\Gamma = 4$ is retained as the best compromise.

\textbf{The Main Issue:} The parameter sweep reveals a structural limitation: while gas pressure matches experiments in the outer layers ($10$ and $20~\mathrm{mm}$), the simulated peak pressure \emph{decreases} at greater depths ($30$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$), contradicting Kalifa et al.~\cite{kalifa2000}.

Physically, this discrepancy suggests that the simulatated high-gas-pressure region is spatially smaller than it should be. The pressure build-up and the formation of the ``moisture clog'' remain concentrated too close to the exposed surface, failing to propagate massive condensation and pressurization into the deeper layers of the concrete core.

\subsection{Secondary adjustments investigated} \label{sec:cal-secondary}
 
To address this depth-dependent pressure decrease and force the moisture clog deeper into the material, several secondary parameters were investigated:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial relative humidity ($RH_0$):} Imposing full saturation ($RH_0 > 0.98$) to increase the vaporization source systematically crashes the solver due to severe capillary pressure gradients (\cref{sec:robustness})
    \item \textbf{Desorption isotherm ($a$):} Adopting a stiffer desorption curve of HPC (\cref{tab:VG}) slightly raises the peak pressure at $20~\mathrm{mm}$ but has no effect on deeper zones.
    \item \textbf{Aging time:} Shorter curing (6 months) compromises numerical stability, while excessive aging unrealistically dries out the core prior to heating via self-desiccation.
    \item \textbf{Vapor mass-transfer coefficient ($h_g$):} In an attempt to artificially trap the gas, the convective exchange coefficient at the heated face was reduced to a minimum. While this approach successfully forced vapor inward and increased $p_g$ in the deeper zones, it requires an artificially large intrinsic permeability ($K_0$) to maintain stability, which contradicts the dense nature of HPC.
\end{itemize}

The outcome of the last attempt is shown in \cref{fig:cal-hg-result}: with $h_g = 0.001$ and a large $K_0 = 2\times10^{-18}~\mathrm{m^2}$, increasing $A_\Gamma$ ($1$ to $4$) does raise the deeper-zone pressures. However, the abnormally high $K_0$ lets gas escape too easily, depressing the overall pressure magnitude.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/chapter4/pg_test10_AK_sweep_hg0.001.png}
  \caption{Gas-pressure histories at $10$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$ for a minimal vapour transfer coefficient $h_g = 0.001$ and a large $K_0 = 2\times10^{-18}~\mathrm{m^2}$, sweeping $A_\Gamma$ from $1$ to $4$.}
  \label{fig:cal-hg-result}
\end{figure}
 
In summary, none of these secondary adjustments -- alone or in combination with $K_0$ and $A_\Gamma$ -- could perfectly reproduce the deep pressure peaks without violating either the physical constraints of the material or the mathematical stability of the solver.

\section{Validation and discussion} \label{sec:validation}

The reference model utilizes the calibrated parameter set optimized primarily for the thermal field and the near-surface gas pressure. Based on the calibration journey, the following critical discussions and validation outcomes are drawn.

    \hline
    Stage & Parameter & Retained value \\
    \hline

                          & Emissivity $\varepsilon$            & $0.6$ \\
    Bulk conduction       & Reference dry conductivity $\lambda_{d0}$ & $1.38~\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}$ \\
                          & Solid specific heat $C_{ps}$        & $960~\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}$ \\

                          & Permeability--hydration coeff.\ $A_\Gamma$ & $4$ \\

  \end{tabular}

\subsection{Temperature} \label{sec:val-temp}
The temperature validation is the in-depth comparison already obtained at the end of the calibration (\cref{fig:cal-depth-final}): with the calibrated set of Table~\ref{tab:calibrated}, the model reproduces the measured temperature histories at all depths within a few degrees, confirming that the thermal sub-problem and its boundary conditions are correctly captured. Importantly, the gas-pressure parameters $K_0$ and $A_\Gamma$ calibrated in the last stage have a negligible effect on the temperature field, so the temperature agreement of \cref{fig:cal-depth-final} remains valid for the final parameter set. The temperature field can therefore be used as a trustworthy loading for the hydric and mechanical sub-problems.

\subsection{Gas pressure} \label{sec:val-pressure}
 
The gas-pressure validation is the comparison obtained with the retained pair
(\cref{fig:cal-pg-compare}). The response \textbf{could not be validated}: even with the calibrated set of Table~\ref{tab:calibrated}, and across the explored parameter space -- $K_0$, $A_\Gamma$, $RH_0$, $h_g$, the desorption parameter $a$ and the aging time -- the model reproduces the pressure only in the outer layers ($10$ and $20~\mathrm{mm}$). In the deeper zones the simulated peak pressure keeps decreasing with depth, while the measurements show the opposite trend, so the high-pressure region remains too concentrated near the exposed surface. 

Additionally, the conduction parameters $C_{ps}$ and $\lambda_{d0}$ retained for the temperature have a negligible effect on the gas pressure. With the fact $K_0$ and $A_\Gamma$ ave a negligible effect on the temperature field, these justify the sequential calibration adopted in this chapter.

 
\subsection{Dicussions} \label{sec:val-dicussion}

Two complementary explanations are proposed for this discrepancy.
\begin{itemize}

  \item The first concerns the experiment itself. Measuring pore pressure requires inserting a gauge into the concrete, which inevitably creates a local discontinuity -- a small cavity surrounded by an already porous and heterogeneous material. It is therefore not certain that these gauges record the true gas pressure of the intact porous network; part of what they measure may reflect the local conditions of the cavity and the surrounding damaged zone rather than the field the model computes. This raises a genuine question about what the deep-zone gauges are actually measuring, and hence about the reference against which the model is being judged.
 
  \item The second concerns the model's formulation and its current scope. The formulation used here is the recent THC model, which is still being developed; its saturation, permeability, and dehydration laws have not yet been fully tuned to the high-temperature behaviour of HPC. Furthermore, while it accounts for microstructural evolution via chemical dehydration (the effective hydration degree $\tilde{\Gamma}$), it inherently excludes mechanical damage (thermally and hydrally induced micro-cracking). In reality, cracking drastically alters the local intrinsic permeability (M~$\rightarrow$~H coupling) and dictates how and when gas pressure is relieved. The fact that the deeper-zone pressures could be raised only through physically unreasonable parameter values (very high $K_0$, minimal $h_g$) suggests that the model is missing a mechanism that redistributes the pressure build-up further inward. Therefore, both a refinement of the THC formulation and the integration of mechanical damage are required before a fully quantitative validation of the gas pressure can be expected.
\end{itemize}

Given the inherent uncertainties in both the non-linear material parameters and the ambiguous experimental gas-pressure data, relying solely on deterministic calibration is insufficient. This robustly justifies the necessity of shifting toward a probabilistic framework, utilizing uncertainty quantification to assess the spalling risk, which will be the core focus of \cref{chapter5}.
