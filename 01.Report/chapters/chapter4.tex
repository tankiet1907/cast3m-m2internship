\draft{This chapter presents the calibration and validation of the coupled thermo-hydral (TH) model implemented in Cast3M against the Kalifa experiment \cite{kalifa2000}. Rather than adjusting every material parameter at once, a \emph{sequential} calibration is adopted that follows the physical hierarchy of the coupled problem: the temperature field is calibrated first and, only once it is reliably reproduced, the gas-pressure response is addressed. The chapter is therefore organised as a calibration ``journey'': from the heated surface inward for the thermal field (Section~\ref{sec:cal-temp}), then from the dominant to the secondary parameters for the hydric field (Section~\ref{sec:cal-pressure}), closing with the validation outcome and its limitations (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).}

\section{Model calibration} \label{sec:calibration}

The accuracy of TH/THC model relies on a large number of material parameters -- intrinsic permeability, thermal conductivity and heat capacity, sorption properties, surface exchange coefficients and a complex set of constitutive laws describing their evolution with temperature and dehydration.

Many critical properties at high-temperature such as the evolution of intrinsic permeability, sorption isotherms are extremely difficult or even impossible to measure directly through experimental techniques and the values often contain uncertainties due to the intrusive nature of the sensors. Furthermore these are not measured directly in the Kalifa campaign.

A pre-determined parameter set therefore cannot be expected to reproduce the experiment. Consequently, a calibration procedure for the numerical models is strictly required to identify, within physically admissible ranges, the parameter set that makes the model reproduce the measured temperature and gas-pressure histories. Only once this reference case is calibrated and validated can the model be trusted for uncertainty studies in \cref{chapter5}.

\subsection{Calibration strategy} \label{sec:cal-strategy}
At the first approximation, the thermal problem is weakly coupled to the hydric one: the temperatur field drives evaporation, dehydration and the gas-pressure build-up, while the effect of the hydric state on the temperature field is relatively small within the experimental range. This hierachy promotes a two-stage calibration process:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Stage~1 -- Temperature.} The temperature evolution is calibrated independently of the pressure field. In this stage, the \emph{surface} (boundary) parameters should be calibrated first, then the \emph{bulk} conduction parameters.
  \item \textbf{Stage~2 -- Gas pressure.} With the reliaably calibrated temperature field as a fixed thermal loading, the hydric parameters gorvening the gas-pressure evolution are adjusted, from the most influential to the secondary ones.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Targets and criteria.}
The calibration targets are the temperature and gas-pressure histories measured at $10$, $20$, $30$, $40$ and $50~\mathrm{mm}$ from the heated face, together with the near-surface temperature recorded at $2~\mathrm{mm}$ (denoted the $0~\mathrm{mm}$ measurement).


\subsection{Automated parameter sweep} \label{sec:cal-automation}
The sweeps are driven by a Python script that launches Cast3M repeatedly, one run per parameter combination, without manual intervention. For each stage the parameter values to be tried are listed explicitly, and \texttt{itertools.product} builds every possible combination of those lists; a single loop then feeds each combination to Cast3M in turn.

This automation relies on two supporting pieces of code. First, the Cast3M input code itself exports the results of each run to a \texttt{.csv} file -- the temperature and gas-pressure histories at the measurement points. Second, a separate Python post-processing script reads these \texttt{.csv} files and plots the computed histories against the experimental measurements \cite{kalifa2000} and also against the results from Dorjan Dauti's thesis \cite{dauti2018}. All combinations then can be visually compared, and the best-fit set is selected by the author based on this comparison.
 
For example, in the gas-pressure calibration stage, the two parameters swept are the intrinsic permeability $K_0$ (denoted \texttt{KINT}) and the permeability--hydration coefficient $A_\Gamma$ (denoted \texttt{AK}), with the trial values:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $K_0 \in \{1\!\times\!10^{-19},\ 2\!\times\!10^{-19},\ 1\!\times\!10^{-20},\
  5\!\times\!10^{-20},\ 7.5\!\times\!10^{-20},\ 8.5\!\times\!10^{-20}\}~\mathrm{m^2}$
  ($6$ values);
  \item $A_\Gamma \in \{3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7,\ 8\}$ ($6$ values).
\end{itemize}
These two lists give $6\times 6 = 36$ parameter pairs, i.e.\ $36$ Cast3M runs. The same driver is reused by simply changing the value lists.

In every stage, a wider range of each parameter is first explored to bracket the admissible values; the search is then narrowed to the few representative values reported in the following sections, which are the ones shown in the figures.

\section{Temperature calibration} \label{sec:cal-temp}
    
\subsection{Surface temperature (\texorpdfstring{$0~\mathrm{mm}$}{0 mm}): boundary heat exchange} \label{sec:cal-surf}
 
The first quantity calibrated is the temperature of the heated face. In the test, heat is supplied by a radiant heater facing the specimen, so the surface energy balance is dominated by radiation and convection:
\begin{equation}
  q_{\text{surf}} = h_c\,(T_\infty - T_s)
  + \varepsilon\,\sigma\,\bigl(T_r^{4} - T_s^{4}\bigr),
  \label{eq:surf-bc}
\end{equation}
where $h_c$ is the convective heat-transfer coefficient, $\varepsilon$ the surface emissivity (radiative coefficient), $\sigma$ the Stefan--Boltzmann constant, $T_r$ the radiant-source temperature and $T_s$ the surface temperature.
 
Because every in-depth temperature is slaved to the surface temperature, the boundary condition~\eqref{eq:surf-bc} is calibrated \emph{first}. The two boundary coefficients $h_c$ and $\varepsilon$ are adjusted so that the computed $0~\mathrm{mm}$ history matches the measured near-surface ($2~\mathrm{mm}$) response -- in particular the initial heating rate and the asymptotic surface temperature. At this point the conduction parameters are held at their reference values; only the energy input at the surface is tuned.

The surface response is calibrated over three values of $h_c$ and three of
$\varepsilon$, giving the $3\times 3$ set of surface-temperature curves shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:cal-surface}. The retained pair is $h_c = [\,\dots\,]$ and
$\varepsilon = [\,\dots\,]$.

Two distinct effects emerge from Fig.~\ref{fig:cal-surface}. The emissivity
$\varepsilon$ is the most sensitive parameter and acts in a strongly non-linear way:
because the radiative term scales as $T^{4}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:surf-bc}, a small
increase in $\varepsilon$ raises the surface temperature substantially, and most
strongly in the high-temperature part of the curve, where radiation dominates the
heat input. The convective coefficient $h_c$ acts more gently and mainly on the
\emph{early} part of the curve: increasing $h_c$ raises the initial heating rate
(the linear $h_c(T_\infty - T_s)$ term is largest while $T_s$ is still low), with
only a slight additional rise in the tail. In short, $\varepsilon$ sets the level of
the high-temperature plateau while $h_c$ controls the initial ramp; tuning the two
together reproduces both ends of the measured surface history.
 
\subsection{In-depth temperature: thermal conductivity and heat capacity} \label{sec:cal-depth}
 
Once the surface temperature is reproduced, the temperature rise at depth
($10$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$) is governed by heat conduction through the material, i.e.\
by the thermal conductivity $\lambda(T)$ and the volumetric heat capacity
$\rho C_p(T)$. The conductivity sets the steepness of the thermal gradient and the
speed of the thermal front, while the heat capacity sets the thermal inertia -- the
time lag of the temperature rise at each depth.
 
A subtlety arises here: adjusting the surface condition in
Section~\ref{sec:cal-surf} changes the energy that actually enters the specimen,
which in turn shifts the in-depth histories. Consequently $\lambda(T)$ and
$\rho C_p(T)$ cannot be calibrated in isolation -- they must be
\emph{re-calibrated} jointly with the surface condition. In practice a short
iterative loop is used:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\lambda(T)$ and $\rho C_p(T)$ are adjusted to match the arrival time and
  slope of the temperature rise at each depth;
  \item $h_c$ and $\varepsilon$ are then re-checked against the surface probe and,
  if necessary, slightly corrected;
\end{enumerate}
and the loop is repeated until the whole set of probes ($0$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$) is
reproduced consistently. As for the surface stage, three values of $\lambda$ and
three of $\rho C_p$ are retained for the final sweep, giving the $3\times 3$ set of
in-depth temperature curves shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cal-temp-depth}.

The two parameters act on different parts of the specimen. The conductivity
$\lambda$ mainly affects the region \emph{closer to the heated face}: it sets how
fast heat is conducted inward. Lowering $\lambda$ slows the inward transfer, so heat
accumulates near the surface and the near-face temperature rises while the deeper
points heat up more slowly; raising $\lambda$ has the opposite effect, transmitting
heat inward and lowering the near-surface temperature. The heat capacity
$\rho C_p$, by contrast, governs the thermal inertia and is felt mostly at the
\emph{deepest points} ($40$ and $50~\mathrm{mm}$): a larger $\rho C_p$ stores more
energy per unit temperature rise, so the same heat flux produces a smaller and more
delayed temperature increase in the core, lowering the internal temperatures.
Adjusting $\lambda$ to match the near-face response and $\rho C_p$ to match the
$40$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$ histories therefore brings the whole depth profile into
agreement.
 
The final temperature agreement is good across all depths
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cal-temp-depth}), which provides a reliable thermal loading for the
second calibration stage.

\section{Gas-pressure calibration} \label{sec:cal-pressure}
\begin{draftblock}

With the temperature field fixed, the gas-pressure evolution is addressed. The pressure build-up behind the moisture clog (Section~\ref{...}) is controlled chiefly by how easily vapour can escape the heated region -- i.e.\ by the permeability -- and by how much water is available to be vaporised.
 
\subsection{Primary parameters: \texorpdfstring{$K_0$}{K0} and \texorpdfstring{$A_\Gamma$}{A_Gamma}} \label{sec:cal-K0-AK}
 
Two parameters dominate the pressure response:
 
\begin{description}
  \item[Intrinsic permeability $K_0$.] It sets the absolute resistance to gas flow. Lowering $K_0$ traps vapour near the hot face and raises the pressure peak; raising it lets the gas escape and flattens the peak. $K_0$ is therefore the primary control on the \emph{magnitude} of the peak.
  \item[Permeability--hydration coefficient $A_\Gamma$ (denoted $A_K$).] It governs the evolution of permeability with the effective hydration degree $\tilde{\Gamma}$ in the law $K(\tilde{\Gamma})$, i.e.\ how the microstructure built up during curing modulates transport. It mainly controls the \emph{aging dependence} of the response and shifts the peak \emph{timing}.
\end{description}
 
% \begin{figure}[h] \centering ... \caption{Sensitivity of the gas-pressure
% history to $K_0$ (left) and $A_\Gamma$ (right) at $10$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$.}
% \label{fig:cal-pressure-primary} \end{figure}
 
The sweep over $(K_0, A_\Gamma)$ shows that the peak amplitude and timing can be brought close to the measured values \emph{individually}, but tuning one quantity to fit the peak degrades the fit elsewhere in the history.
 
\subsection{Secondary adjustments investigated} \label{sec:cal-secondary}
 
Since the two primary parameters alone did not yield a satisfactory fit, several additional quantities were investigated:
 
\begin{description}
  \item[Initial relative humidity $RH_0$ (initial saturation).] It fixes the initial liquid-water content and hence the amount of water available for vaporisation. A higher $RH_0$ feeds a larger and later pressure peak.
  \item[Vapour mass-transfer (convection) coefficient.] The boundary exchange coefficient for vapour at the heated face controls how readily vapour leaves the surface; it mainly affects the early pressurisation and the near-surface peak.
  \item[Aging / curing time.] Through the hydration degree it changes the initial porosity, saturation and permeability, and thus the entire pressure history; longer aging densifies the microstructure and tends to raise the peaks.
\end{description}
 
Each of these was found to influence the response in a physically expected direction, but none of them -- alone or in combination with $K_0$ and $A_\Gamma$ -- resolved the qualitative mismatch discussed below.

\end{draftblock}

\subsection{Temperature} \label{sec:val-temp}
 
The calibrated model reproduces the measured temperature histories at all depths within a few degrees, confirming that the thermal sub-problem and its boundary conditions are correctly captured. The temperature field can therefore be used as a trustworthy loading for the hydric and mechanical sub-problems.
 
\subsection{Gas pressure}
\label{sec:val-pressure}
 
The gas-pressure response, by contrast, \textbf{could not be validated}. Across the explored parameter space -- $K_0$, $A_\Gamma$, $RH_0$, the vapour transfer coefficient and the aging time -- no single parameter set reproduces \emph{simultaneously} the magnitude, the timing and, above all, the strong post-peak decay of the measured pressure. With the new (THC) saturation and permeability formulation, the model systematically:
\begin{itemize}
  \item overestimates the pressurisation phase before the peak, and
  \item fails to reproduce the sharp pressure relief observed after the peak.
\end{itemize}
 
% \begin{figure}[h] \centering ... \caption{Computed vs.\ measured gas-pressure
% histories at $10$--$50~\mathrm{mm}$: the model captures the pressurisation trend
% but not the post-peak decay.} \label{fig:val-pressure} \end{figure}
 
This limitation is consistent with the physics omitted from the present TH model. The measured pressure decay is largely driven by the cracking and mechanical damage that develop near the hot face, which sharply increase the local permeability and let the trapped gas escape. Because the present formulation does not include the damage--permeability coupling (Eq.~\ref{eq:K-damage}), it cannot capture this relief, and consequently the gas-pressure field cannot be validated against the Kalifa data using the TH/THC formulation alone.
 
\subsection{Conclusions and implications} \label{sec:val-conclusions}

\review{This is wrong conclustion} 
Two conclusions follow from the calibration journey:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The temperature field -- the loading for the hydric and mechanical sub-problems -- is reliably reproduced, once the surface boundary exchange and then the bulk conduction parameters are calibrated in sequence.
  \item A quantitative match of the gas pressure is \emph{not} achievable with the new $p_g$ formulation on its own: it is necessary but not sufficient. Capturing the experimental pressure -- and in particular its decay -- requires activating the mechanical (damage) coupling that increases permeability through cracking.
\end{enumerate}
 
This motivates the coupled THM analysis carried out in the following chapter, where the damage--permeability coupling is reintroduced.
