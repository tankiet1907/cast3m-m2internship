\section{The Kalifa experiment} \label{sec:kalifa}

\paragraph{Purpose and selection rationale.} The campaign of Kalifa et al.~\cite{kalifa2000} is one of the first and most widely recognized small-scale tests designed to provide a comprehensive quantitative dataset for understanding concrete spalling. It continuously and \emph{simultaneously} measures the temperature, the gas (pore) pressure at several depths, and the global mass loss of a concrete specimen subjected to a severe one-dimensional thermal load. Consequently, it has become a highly popular experimental benchmark for validating coupled numerical codes.

This specific experiment was deliberately chosen as the primary validation benchmark for two main reasons. First, it provides the essential macroscopic variables required to assess the pore-pressure build-up mechanism. Second, the Kalifa test was extensively analyzed and simulated in Dauti's thesis \cite{dauti2018} using a standard Thermo-Hydric (TH) model. By adopting the same reference experiment, the present study can rigorously verify the newly implemented Thermo-Hydro-Chemical (THC) model by comparing its predictions not only against the raw experimental measurements but also against the established TH coupled results from Dauti.

\paragraph{Set-up and instrumentation.}
A prismatic specimen of $30\times30\times12~\si{\centi\meter\cubed}$ is heated on one $30\times30~\si{\centi\meter\squared}$ face by a radiant heater (up to \SI{5}{\kilo\watt}, \SI{800}{\celsius}) placed \SI{3}{\centi\meter} above the surface. The lateral faces are insulated with porous ceramic blocks, so heat and mass transfer are essentially one-dimensional. The specimen rests on a balance recording its mass. Five gauges measure pressure and temperature at \SIlist{10;20;30;40;50}{\milli\meter} from the heated face, and a sixth tube measures the surface temperature at \SI{2}{\milli\meter} depth.

\paragraph{Concrete mix.}
Only the high-performance concrete (HPC, mix~M100-C) is retained for the modelling: it exhibits the most pronounced thermo-hydral response (measured gas-pressure peak $\approx\SI{40}{\bar}$) and an initial liquid saturation $S_{l,0}\approx0.77$. It uses a CEM\,I 52.5 cement blended with silica fume at a low water/binder ratio, giving the high compactness and low permeability responsible for the strong pressurisation. The detailed mixture proportions, after Kalifa et al.~\cite{kalifa2000}, are deferred to \cref{appendixA}, while the lumped quantities fed to Powers' model ($c$, aggregate, $w/c$, $s/c$) are directly listed in \cref{tab:params}.

\paragraph{Heating scenario and measured data.} In the experiment the specimen is heated on one face by a radiant heater applied as a step signal, the heater temperature being set to one of \SIlist{450;600;800}{\celsius}. The \SI{600}{\celsius} test is retained as the reference loading, since it preserves the quasi-1-D transfer condition while developing a complete pressurisation and relief cycle. 

The three measured quantities — temperature, gas pressure at various depths, and global mass loss — serve as the validation targets for this work. They are not plotted here: to keep the experimental curves together with the simulated response, they are reported directly alongside the numerical results during the calibration phase (\cref{chapter4}).

\section{Numerical model} \label{sec:num-model}
\paragraph{Scope of the numerical simulation.} While the theoretical THM framework is detailed in \cref{appendixA}, this numerical application is restricted to the Thermo-Hydro-Chemical (THC) model. The mechanical module is deliberately excluded because the Kalifa experiment provides only temperature and gas-pressure data, lacking the mechanical measurements (e.g., strains or damage) required for validation.

\paragraph{Geometry and mesh.} The test is modelled in 2D using a $120\times120~\mathrm{mm^2}$ cross-section (\cref{fig:setup}). The top face is heated, the bottom face is cooled, and the lateral faces are fully insulated to reproduce a quasi-1D transfer. The domain is meshed with $24\times24$ four-node quadrilateral (\texttt{QUA4}) elements. The field variables are extracted along the vertical centerline at depths corresponding to the experimental gauges ($0, 10, 20, 30, 40, \text{ and } 50~\mathrm{mm}$).

\paragraph{Material parameters.} The reference parameters for the HPC M100 mix are summarised in \cref{tab:params}. These inputs feed directly into the THC constitutive laws rather than relying on empirical temperature-dependent functions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hydration kinetics:} Parameters such as activation energy and latent heat explicitly drive the curing phase (\cref{sec:affinity}).
    \item \textbf{Permeability evolution:} The intrinsic permeability $K$ is directly coupled to the effective hydration degree $\tilde{\Gamma}$ (\cref{sec:perm-law}):
    \begin{equation}
        K(\tilde{\Gamma}) = K_0 \cdot 10^{A_\Gamma (1 - \tilde{\Gamma})^{B_K}}
        \label{eq:ch3-perm}
    \end{equation}
    where $K_0$ is the initial permeability, and $A_\Gamma$, $B_K$ control the permeability increase as C-S-H decomposes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Thermal input and boundary conditions.} The boundary set-up and the radiator temperature histories follow the modelling of Dauti~\cite{dauti2018} (\cref{fig:setup}). On the hot face a combined convection--radiation condition (See \cref{eq:thermal_bc}) is imposed:
\begin{equation}
    q_{\mathrm{hot}} = -\lambda \frac{\partial T}{\partial n}\Big|_{\mathrm{hot}} = h^{\mathrm{hot}}(T_\infty - T_s) + \varepsilon \sigma \big(T_\infty^4 - T_s^4\big),
    \label{eq:heated-face}
\end{equation}
with the initial reference values $h^{\mathrm{hot}}=\SI{8}{\watt\per\meter\squared\per\kelvin}$, emissivity $\varepsilon=0.7$, and a prescribed radiator temperature $T_\infty(t)$ that rises to $\approx\SI{400}{\celsius}$ at the onset of the accident and then increases quasi-linearly to $\approx\SI{466}{\celsius}$ over the $\approx\SI{240}{\minute}$ accident (\cref{fig:setup}b); the radiator is switched off afterwards. The cold face exchanges heat by convection only ($h^{\mathrm{cold}}=\SI{8.3}{\watt\per\meter\squared\per\kelvin}$, $T_\infty=\SI{20}{\celsius}$). On both faces the gas pressure is fixed at atmospheric ($p_g=p_{\mathrm{atm}}$) and a convective vapour flux $q_w=h_g(\rho_{v}^{s}-\rho_{v}^{\infty})$ is applied.

The mass-transfer coefficient $h_g$ increasing from $0$ (sealed) to $\SI{2e-3}{}$ (drying) and $\SI{1.8e-2}{\meter\per\second}$ (accident). It is important to distinguish these transfer coefficients: while the convective heat transfer coefficients ($h^{\mathrm{hot}}, h^{\mathrm{cold}}$) govern the exchange of thermal energy across the boundary, the mass-transfer coefficient ($h_g$) specifically dictates the rate at which water vapour escapes from the porous network to the surrounding environment.

\section{Application of the THC model} \label{sec:thc-application}

\subsection{Numerical implementation and robustness} \label{sec:robustness}
The THC model is implemented within the open-source finite element code \textsc{Cast3M}. However, standard \textsc{Cast3M} procedures do not natively support the complex constitutive evolution laws and the highly coupled convective mass flux boundary conditions required for modelling concrete at high temperatures. To overcome these limitations, the implementation relies on custom dedicated operators and modified source files (developed by G.~Sciumè and S.~Dal~Pont). 

Furthermore, the strongly non-linear nature of the problem frequently causes the Newton-Raphson solver (the \texttt{PASAPAS} integrator) to diverge at extreme temperatures. To restore convergence and ensure numerical stability, the following measures are investigated and implemented:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fully implicit time integration ($\theta=1$):} Applied to efficiently damp non-linear oscillations and ensure solver convergence (\cref{sec:temporal_discret}).
    \item \textbf{Drastic reduction of the time step ($\Delta t$):} Reduced to as low as \SI{1}{\second} during peak thermal gradients to compensate for the implicit scheme's lower accuracy and guarantee stability.
    \item \textbf{Linear elements (\texttt{QUA4}):} Strictly retained over higher-order elements to prevent unphysical spatial oscillations (e.g., negative gas pressures) in the highly non-linear multiphase flow.
    \item \textbf{Regular unrefined mesh ($24 \times 24$):} Deliberately kept uniform to isolate physical instabilities from meshing-induced errors.
    \item \textbf{Artificial initial relative humidity ($\mathrm{RH}_0 = 0.5$):} Imposed during the early-age curing phase to bypass immediate solver divergence, without altering the final high-temperature response.
    \item \textbf{Tuning thermal radiation:} Emissivity is reduced to $\epsilon=0.7$ (from 0.85 in \cite{dauti2018}) to prevent excessive surface overheating and keep temperatures tractable.
    \item \textbf{Balancing permeability parameters:} The starting calibration pair is cautiously set to $K_0 = 2\times 10^{-19}~\mathrm{m^2}$ and $A_\Gamma = 3$ to avoid numerical singularities caused by extreme pressure gradients.
\end{itemize}

All these robustness measures and numerical stabilization strategies were thoroughly investigated and implemented by the author, requiring a substantial investment of time and effort to successfully debug and execute the highly non-linear THC simulations without divergence.

\subsection{Simulation timeline and phases} \label{sec:simulation-phases}
A key feature of this THC application is that the hygro-chemical state preceding the fire accident is explicitly simulated rather than arbitrarily prescribed. The full history is executed via staged \texttt{PASAPAS} computations in three consecutive phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Early age (0--3 days):} The concrete undergoes sealed, adiabatic hydration starting from $T_0=\SI{20}{\celsius}$, $p_{g,0}=\SI{101325}{\pascal}$, and $\Gamma_0=0$.
    \item \textbf{Aging and drying (3 days--1 year):} The specimen is exposed to heat and moisture convection until the external humidity drops to $0.50$.
    \item \textbf{Accident phase (0--240 mins):} The heating ramp is applied to the hot face (convection and radiation). The temperature rises immediately, triggering the dehydration of C-S-H and the subsequent critical pore-pressure build-up.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Cooling phase omission:} Although the Kalifa experimental campaign continued to record temperature and gas-pressure data during the cooling phase (from $240$ up to $600~\mathrm{minutes}$), this final phase is \emph{not} modelled in the present work. Since the primary objective of this study is to investigate the mechanisms triggering explosive spalling, which inherently occur during the severe heating phase. The numerical simulation is deliberately stopped at $t=\SI{240}{\minute}$. Consequently, the experimental cooling data is excluded from the comparative plots in the calibration phase (\cref{chapter4}).
