\section{Stochastic finite element framework} \label{sec:stochastic-framework}
\subsection{Motivation}
In civil engineering, material properties such as permeability, thermal conductivity, or compressive strength are never perfectly known due to the inherent heterogeneity of concrete \cite{baroth2011,delarrard2010}. Evaluating structural safety using purely deterministic models---which assign a single fixed value to each parameter---is therefore often insufficient, as it cannot capture the realistic scatter of the structural response or quantify the likelihood of failure.

This limitation is particularly critical for predicting explosive spalling, a highly localized failure driven by spatial fluctuations of material properties (e.g., micro-defects) and the unpredictable nature of extreme thermal loads. To explicitly quantify this risk, a \emph{stochastic} framework must be adopted, where material parameters are treated as random variables or spatial random fields. 

\textbf{The comprehensive theoretical background regarding the formal classification of uncertainties (aleatoric vs. epistemic), as well as the mathematical definitions linking these core probabilistic concepts to the utilized simulation methods, are deferred to \cref{sec:uncertainty-theory}.}

\subsection{Continuous stochastic workflow}
To handle uncertainties, several numerical tools (e.g., LHS, Monte Carlo, PCE, KDE, Gaussian Quadrature) are employed in a continuous workflow based on the Probability Density Function (PDF) and Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input:} Each uncertain parameter (e.g., permeability, $w/c$) is defined by a prescribed PDF.
    \item \textbf{Sampling (LHS \& MC):} Discrete values are drawn from these distributions. While Monte Carlo (MC) samples randomly, Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) uses the CDF to ensure an even, space-filling sampling.
    \item \textbf{Propagation (PCE):} To avoid thousands of expensive FEM simulations, Polynomial Chaos Expansion (PCE) acts as a surrogate model to rapidly transform the input PDFs into the output PDF.
    \item \textbf{Output and Risk (KDE \& Quadrature):} The response PDF is reconstructed using either Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) from sampled data or Gaussian Quadrature via statistical moments. The probability of failure ($P_f$) is then evaluated directly by integrating the tail of this PDF beyond a critical threshold.
\end{itemize}

Within this general workflow, the spatial dimension of the uncertainty is treated at two levels:
\begin{description}
    \item[Random Variable (RV).] Each uncertain property is described by a single probability distribution applied uniformly over the whole specimen. This approach is used for the sensitivity (\cref{sec:sensitivity}) and reliability (\cref{sec:reliability}) analyses.
    \item[Random Field (RF).] The property varies spatially across the domain, subject to a correlation structure. This level is used for the spatial heterogeneity study in \cref{sec:spatial-effect}.
\end{description}

\section{Uncertain inputs and quantity of interest} \label{sec:uncertain-params}
 
\subsection{Two separate analyses} \label{sec:two-analyses}
 
The uncertainty is propagated through \emph{two independent studies} rather than a single combined one:
 
\begin{description}
  \item[Analysis~A --- intrinsic permeability $K_0$.] A \emph{direct} uncertainty on a transport property, identified in \cref{chapter4} as the dominant control on the gas-pressure magnitude.
  \item[Analysis~B --- mix composition.] An \emph{upstream} uncertainty on the mix-design proportions, introduced at its physical source and propagated through Powers' model (\cref{chapter2}, \cref{appendixA}), which converts the proportions into porosity, hydration degree, sorption and the resulting transport properties.
\end{description}
 
The two are kept apart for two reasons. First, they are of different nature --- a material-property scatter on one side, an upstream design imprecision on the other --- and mixing them would blur the interpretation. Second, $K_0$ is known from \cref{chapter4} to dominate the pressure response; pooling it with the four mix proportions in a single variance decomposition would let its variance swamp the Sobol indices (\cref{sec:sobol}) and \emph{mask} the relative importance \emph{among} the mix proportions. Separating the analyses lets each source be characterised on its own terms.

\textbf{The detailed physical and mathematical justifications for selecting the Lognormal law for permeability, the Normal law for batching weights, and the Uniform law for design ratios are deferred to \cref{sec:distribution-justification}.}

The four mix proportions are taken as mutually independent design choices; their physical coupling (a given $w/c$ and $s/c$ jointly set porosity and permeability) is produced \emph{inside} the model by Powers' stoichiometry, not imposed on the inputs. The \textsc{OpenTURNS} declaration is given in \cref{tab:random-variables}.

\subsection{Quantity of interest and limit state} \label{sec:qoi}
 
Both analyses share the same scalar quantity of interest (QoI): the peak gas pressure at the $10~\mathrm{mm}$ depth:
\begin{equation}
    p_g^{10} = \max_t \, p_g(x = 10~\mathrm{mm},\,t).
    \label{eq:qoi}
\end{equation}

Following the general reliability framework defined in \cref{sec:limit-state}, the specific limit-state function for this study is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    G(\mathbf{X}) = p_{\mathrm{crit}} - p_g^{10}(\mathbf{X}),
    \label{eq:ch5-limit-state}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{X}$ is the input vector of the analysis considered and $p_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is the critical pressure tied to the temperature-degraded tensile strength. Failure corresponds to $G(\mathbf{X}) \le 0$.

\section{Uncertainty propagation with \texorpdfstring{\textsc{OpenTURNS}}{OpenTURNS}} \label{sec:propagation}
 
Uncertainty quantification is carried out with the \textsc{OpenTURNS} library, reusing the Python automation layer built for the calibration sweeps of \cref{sec:cal-automation}. There, \texttt{itertools.product} is also reused for a probabilistic design.

Besides, extra steps is required for the automated stochastic analyses:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the distribution(\cref{tab:random-variables}), using \texttt{ComposedDistribution}.
  \item Generate an LHS design of $N$ samples (\cref{sec:lhs-theory}).
  \item Python script for post-process and ploting: correlation, Sobol indices, response PDF.
\end{enumerate}
 
 
To reduce computational cost compared to standard Monte Carlo, Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) is employed via OpenTURNS. The theoretical details of LHS are presented in \cref{sec:lhs-theory}.

\section{Sensitivity analysis} \label{sec:sensitivity}

\subsection{Analysis A --- permeability $K_0$} \label{sec:sens-K0}
With $A_\Gamma$ held constant to isolate the magnitude-driving uncertainty, Analysis A evaluates how $p_g^{10}$ responds to $K_0$. Since there is only one random variable, a Sobol decomposition is not meaningful ($S_1=S_{T}=1$). The results highlight a strongly negative correlation: lower permeability traps more vapour, which results in a higher pressure peak (\cref{fig:sensA-corr,fig:sensA-ci}). 

To interpret the plots and charts in this chapter, the following terminology is used:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baseline vs. Sim:} \textbf{Baseline} is the single deterministic run using the calibrated parameters from \cref{chapter4}. \textbf{Sim} (or Sim Mean) is the average response of all stochastic simulations from \textsc{OpenTURNS}.
    \item \textbf{95\% Confidence Intervals (CI):} On the transient curves, the shaded bands represent the physical dispersion ($95\%$ probability zone). On the Sobol indices, the error bars indicate the statistical estimation error (bootstrap).
\end{itemize}
The theoretical background on these stochastic methods and confidence levels is detailed in \cref{sec:mc-lhs}.

\textbf{Conclusions.} The sensitivity analyses yield the following key insights:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Permeability ($K_0$) is the absolute dominant factor (Analysis A):} As shown in \cref{fig:sensA-corr}, $K_0$ exhibits a nearly deterministic, negative correlation with the peak pressure (Pearson/Spearman $\approx -0.98$), confirming it as the primary controlling input.
    \item \textbf{Water/cement ratio ($w/c$) governs the mix composition (Analysis B):} Both correlation (\cref{fig:sensB-corr}, $\approx 0.93$) and Sobol indices (\cref{fig:sensB-sobol1}, $S_{T} \approx 0.58$) identify $w/c$ as the most influential mix parameter. Physically, a higher $w/c$ provides more free water to vaporise and an opener pore structure, which directly raises $p_g^{10}$ (consistent with \cref{sec:cal-secondary}).
    \item \textbf{Secondary mix parameters and weak interactions:} Cement ($S_{T} \approx 0.25$) and aggregate ($S_{T} \approx 0.15$) have moderate effects, while $s/c$ is entirely negligible ($<0.01$). Because first- and total-order Sobol indices nearly coincide, parameter interactions are weak (\cref{fig:sensB-sobol2}), meaning the response is essentially additive.
    \item \textbf{Low risk from mix uncertainty:} Overall, the mix-induced scatter is remarkably small (mean approximately $p_g^{10}=2.1~\mathrm{MPa}$). Consequently, Analysis B yields a negligible failure probability in \cref{sec:reliability}, and non-influential parameters like $s/c$ can safely be fixed at their mean values.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reliability analysis} \label{sec:reliability}

To quantify the spalling risk, the discrete structural responses are transformed into continuous Probability Density Functions (PDFs) using \textsc{OpenTURNS}. The probability of failure ($P_f$) is then evaluated using two distinct strategies depending on the analysis type:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Kernel Density Estimation (Analyses A, C1, C2):} For single random variables or spatial random fields, KDE is used to directly smooth the raw \textsc{Cast3M} outputs into a continuous PDF. 
    \item \textbf{Monte Carlo on PCE (Analysis B):} For the multivariable mix study, relying on only 30 LHS samples yields inaccurate tail estimations. Instead, a massive Monte Carlo simulation ($10^5$ realizations) is executed on the computationally inexpensive Polynomial Chaos Expansion (PCE) surrogate model to evaluate $P_f$ accurately.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Critical threshold definition:} It is important to note that there is currently no universally accepted, specific gas-pressure threshold that dictates explosive spalling. In this study, the critical threshold at the $10~\mathrm{mm}$ depth is arbitrarily set to $p_{\mathrm{crit}} = 2.5~\mathrm{MPa}$. The primary objective of this section is not to establish an absolute safety criterion, but rather to demonstrate the stochastic methodology---specifically, how input uncertainties propagate into the reconstructed PDFs (\cref{fig:reliability-A,fig:reliability-B}) to evaluate structural reliability.

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Analysis B (\cref{fig:reliability-B}):} The distribution is narrower (confined between $1.8$ and $2.4~\mathrm{MPa}$), meaning no sampled realisation reaches $p_{\mathrm{crit}}$ ($P_f \approx 0$).
\end{itemize}
This confirms that the intrinsic permeability $K_0$ is by far the dominant source of spalling risk.

\section{Effect of spatial heterogeneity on structural safety} \label{sec:spatial-effect}
 
The analyses above treat the permeability as a single random variable, constant over the specimen in a given realisation. Real concrete varies from point to point, and local weak zones --- not average properties --- govern failure. The intrinsic permeability $K_{int}$, the most spatially variable and most influential property (\cref{sec:sens-K0}), is therefore additionally described as a \emph{random field} and compared with the homogeneous description. The same lognormal marginal as in Analysis~A is used (mean $=K_0^{\mathrm{cal}}=8.5\times10^{-20}~\si{\square\meter}$, $\mathrm{CoV}=0.30$, calibrated in \cref{chapter4}).
 
\subsection{A simplified isothermal poro-mechanical model} \label{sec:spatial-model}
Propagating a spatially heterogeneous random field through the fully coupled, highly non-linear Thermo-Hygro-Chemical (THC) model presents severe computational challenges. Instead, a \emph{weakly coupled isothermal poro-mechanical model} is adopted to compare the stochastic methods efficiently. 

The test case simulates the convective drying of a $10 \times 10 \times 10$~\text{cm} concrete cube (1/8 modelled by symmetry) at a constant room temperature ($T=20^\circ\mathrm{C}$). By holding the temperature constant at room conditions ($T=20^\circ\mathrm{C}$), this weak coupling isolates the purely hydromechanical response as the relative humidity drops from $90\%$ to $50\%$.

The intrinsic permeability $K_0$ is treated as the spatially variable input. The primary output of interest is the \emph{longitudinal shrinkage strain} induced strictly by capillary forces ($p_s = -S_l(P_c) P_c$). Detailed constitutive equations, mesh properties, and deterministic parameters are documented in \cref{sec:simplified-model-inputs}.

\subsection{Stochastic propagation methods} \label{sec:spatial-methods}
 
The uncertainty of the intrinsic permeability ($K_0$) is propagated using three distinct stochastic approaches, all detailed in \cref{appendixB}:
 
\begin{description}
    \item[Analysis C1 (OpenTURNS + LHS):] Homogeneous RV baseline (30 samples).
    \item[Analysis C2 (Cast3M \texttt{ALEA}):] Heterogeneous Random Field using the Turning Bands Method (\cref{sec:tbm}) with a spatial correlation length $L_c = 0.02$ m (30 samples).
    \item[Analysis C3 (Gaussian Quadrature):] Homogeneous deterministic integration using \texttt{QUADRATU} (7 points) (\cref{sec:quadrature}).
\end{description}

In Analysis C2, because standard \textsc{Cast3M} functions cannot automatically assemble matrices with continuously varying properties across elements, a \emph{multizone approach} is utilized: the local matrix $\mathbf{K}_i$ is constructed individually for each element $i$ and then explicitly assembled into the global matrix.

In Analysis C3, while \textsc{Cast3M} natively provides \texttt{PARASTAT} to automatically compute statistical moments and \texttt{PROBDENS} to output PDF/CDF curves, the Pearson type III PDF reconstruction was ultimately re-implemented in Python. This choice bypasses \textsc{Cast3M}'s limited visualization capabilities, ensuring high-quality plots and a consistent graphical comparison against the LHS and random field methods.

\textbf{Conclusions.} The comparative study yields three main insights:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quadrature vs. LHS (Homogeneous):} Although computationally cheaper, the 7-point Gaussian quadrature (C3) predicts a more critical shrinkage response than the 30-sample LHS baseline (C1), despite both assuming a uniform permeability.
    \item \textbf{Random Field is the most critical (Heterogeneous):} Analysis C2 produces the highest peak shrinkage strains and the widest probability tail. Physically, local low-permeability zones restrict moisture transport and severely concentrate capillary deformations.
    \item \textbf{Risk underestimation:} While 30 samples is a pragmatic choice due to high computational costs, the significant gap between heterogeneous (C2) and homogeneous (C1, C3) results clearly proves that a simple uniform random-variable assumption dangerously underestimates the structural risk.
\end{itemize}
Theoretical details regarding the \texttt{ALEA} construction, quadrature operators, and convergence are provided in \cref{appendixB}.
