\section{Sources of uncertainty and concrete heterogeneity}

\subsection{Motivation for probabilistic assessment}

In civil engineering, a structure is considered safe when it remains fit for its intended use throughout its service life \cite{baroth2011}. Deterministic models have traditionally been used to evaluate structural safety. However, the gap between a real physical system and a simplified numerical model, along with the unforeseeable nature of future loads and environmental conditions, inherently leads to uncertainty \cite{baroth2023}.

To manage these risks, modern engineering codes such as the Eurocodes adopt a probabilistic format \cite{baroth2011}. This allows the safety of a structure to be explicitly quantified through its probability of failure $P_f$ (\cref{sec:pf-beta}) and its reliability $R = 1 - P_f$. By introducing random variables into mechanical models and propagating them through structural analyses (\cref{sec:stochastic-framework}), objective decisions can be made based on both the probability of an event and the severity of its consequences \cite{baroth2023}.

\subsection{Classification of uncertainties}

According to reliability theory, uncertainties can be classified into two categories \cite{baroth2011,baroth2023}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aleatoric uncertainties} arise from the natural randomness of the environment and the spatial or temporal variability of material properties. They cannot be reduced; they can only be quantified and accounted for in the design process \cite{baroth2023}.

    \item \textbf{Epistemic uncertainties} stem from incomplete knowledge or insufficient data. They include \emph{model uncertainties} (discrepancy between mathematical models and actual physical processes) and \emph{statistical uncertainties} (limited or inaccurate data). Unlike aleatoric uncertainties, they can be reduced by improving models and gathering more data \cite{baroth2023}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concrete heterogeneity and material variability}

A critical source of aleatoric uncertainty is the inherent heterogeneity of concrete \cite{baroth2023,delarrard2010}. Its mechanical properties depend on the formation history, casting conditions, internal chemical reactions, and long-term degradation processes \cite{delarrard2010}.

This variability manifests as \emph{continuous} spatial fluctuations (e.g.\ Young's modulus, permeability, porosity) and \emph{discrete} defects (e.g.\ micro-cracks, capillary networks) \cite{baroth2023}. Consequently, material parameters must be modelled as random variables or random fields rather than deterministic values (\cref{sec:stochastic-framework}).

\section{Stochastic finite element framework} \label{sec:stochastic-framework}
In structural engineering, material properties such as the Young's modulus, permeability, or thermal conductivity are never perfectly known. As noted by \cite{delarrard2010}, even under strict quality control the compressive strength of high-performance concrete can range from 62 to~100~MPa. Two approaches exist to handle this uncertainty:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \emph{deterministic} analysis assigns a single fixed value to each parameter and produces a unique structural response; it cannot, by itself, quantify the likelihood that a design limit will be exceeded.
    \item A \emph{stochastic} analysis, on the other hand, treats uncertain parameters as random quantities, propagates this uncertainty through the model, and delivers statistical information---mean, variance, probability of failure---about the structural response.
\end{enumerate}

Two levels of description are used in this work; their full definitions, moments and simulation methods are given in \cref{sec:rv-rf} and summarised in \cref{tab:rv-vs-rf}.
 
\begin{description}
  \item[Random variable.] Each uncertain property is described by a single distribution (PDF/CDF, mean, standard deviation, CoV; \cref{sec:random-variables}) applied uniformly over the whole specimen in a given realisation. This is the level used for the sensitivity (\cref{sec:sensitivity}) and reliability (\cref{sec:reliability}) analyses; the uncertainty is propagated either by Latin Hypercube sampling (\cref{sec:propagation}) or by Gaussian quadrature (\cref{sec:quadrature}).
  \item[Random field.] The property varies from point to point, subject to a spatial correlation structure characterised by a correlation length $L_c$ (\cref{sec:random-fields,sec:correlation}). Realisations are generated by the Turning Bands Method and propagated by Monte Carlo (\cref{sec:monte-carlo}); this level is used for the spatial-heterogeneity study of \cref{sec:spatial-effect}.
\end{description}
 
A random variable lets strong zones compensate weak ones, whereas a random field allows local weak zones to govern failure --- the distinction examined in \cref{sec:spatial-effect}.

\section{Uncertain inputs and quantity of interest} \label{sec:uncertain-params}
 
\subsection{Two separate analyses} \label{sec:two-analyses}
 
The uncertainty is propagated through \emph{two independent studies} rather than a single combined one:
 
\begin{description}
  \item[Analysis~A --- intrinsic permeability $K_0$.] A \emph{direct} uncertainty on a transport property, identified in \cref{chapter4} as the dominant control on the gas-pressure magnitude.
  \item[Analysis~B --- mix composition.] An \emph{upstream} uncertainty on the mix-design proportions, introduced at its physical source and propagated through Powers' model (\cref{chapter2}, \cref{appendixA}), which converts the proportions into porosity, hydration degree, sorption and the resulting transport properties.
\end{description}
 
The two are kept apart for two reasons. First, they are of different nature --- a material-property scatter on one side, an upstream design imprecision on the other --- and mixing them would blur the interpretation. Second, $K_0$ is known from \cref{chapter4} to dominate the pressure response; pooling it with the four mix proportions in a single variance decomposition would let its variance swamp the Sobol indices and \emph{mask} the relative importance \emph{among} the mix proportions. Separating the analyses lets each source be characterised on its own terms.
 
\subsection{Choice of probability distributions} \label{sec:distributions}
 
Three distribution families are used (\cref{tab:random-variables}), each chosen to reflect the \emph{nature} of the corresponding uncertainty rather than by convenience; the formal definitions are given in \cref{sec:random-variables}.
 
\begin{description}
  \item[Lognormal --- permeability $K_0$.] The intrinsic permeability is strictly positive and varies by multiplicative factors: it is known only to within an order of magnitude and its scatter is naturally expressed as a ratio, not an additive offset. A lognormal law guarantees $K_0>0$ for every sample and reproduces this multiplicative, positively skewed variability; it is the standard model for the permeability of concrete \cite{delarrard2010,sudret2000}. It is also consistent with the random-field generator of \cref{sec:spatial-effect}: the Cast3M \texttt{ALEA} operator produces a Gaussian field that is exponentiated into a lognormal one (\cref{sec:lognormal-transformation}), so the random-variable and random-field descriptions of $K_0$ share the same marginal.
  \item[Normal --- cement content $c$ and aggregate content $agg$.] The batched masses (in \si{\kilogram\per\cubic\meter}) are controlled by the mixing plant around a target value. Their variability is the accumulation of many small, independent dosing and weighing errors, which by the central-limit theorem tends to a Gaussian centred on the nominal dosage. The scatter is small relative to the mean ($\mathrm{CoV}\approx 4\%$ for $c$, $\approx 3\%$ for $agg$), so the symmetric Normal law describes the batch-to-batch dispersion faithfully and the probability of a physically meaningless (negative) sample is negligible.
  \item[Uniform --- ratios $w/c$ and $s/c$.] For the water/cement and silica/cement ratios, what is known is not a measured central tendency but an \emph{admissible design window}: an HPC of this type is specified to lie within $w/c\in[0.30,0.38]$ and $s/c\in[0.08,0.12]$. When only the bounds are known and there is no basis to prefer any value inside them, the maximum-entropy (least-informative) choice is the uniform distribution \cite{baroth2023}. It models the \emph{epistemic} imprecision of the as-specified mix rather than a measurement scatter, and it keeps every sample strictly within the physically admissible window, avoiding tails that would push Powers' model or the solver outside its valid range.
\end{description}
 
The four mix proportions are taken as mutually independent design choices; their physical coupling (a given $w/c$ and $s/c$ jointly set porosity and permeability) is produced \emph{inside} the model by Powers' stoichiometry, not imposed on the inputs. The \textsc{OpenTURNS} declaration is given in \cref{tab:random-variables}.

\subsection{Quantity of interest and limit state} \label{sec:qoi}
 
Both analyses share the same scalar quantity of interest (QoI): the \emph{peak} gas pressure of the $10~\mathrm{mm}$ history,
\begin{equation}
  p_g^{10} \;=\; \max_t \, p_g(x = 10~\mathrm{mm},\,t).
  \label{eq:qoi}
\end{equation}
This near-surface gauge is the most critical location for thermo-hydral spalling, it was a calibration target in \cref{chapter4}, and it is the one depth the model reproduces reliably (the deeper probes are not validated, \cref{sec:val-pressure}), so it is a trustworthy and experimentally anchored output. Spalling is expressed by the limit-state function (\cref{sec:limit-state})
\begin{equation}
  G(\mathbf{X}) = p_{\mathrm{crit}} - p_g^{10}(\mathbf{X}),
  \label{eq:limit-state}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{X}$ is the input vector of the analysis considered ($K_0$ in A; $(c,agg,w/c,s/c)$ in B) and $p_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is the critical pressure tied to the temperature-degraded tensile strength. Failure corresponds to $G\le 0$.

\section{Uncertainty propagation with \texorpdfstring{\textsc{OpenTURNS}}{OpenTURNS}} \label{sec:propagation}
 
\subsection{Coupling \textsc{OpenTURNS} to the Cast3M solver}
 
Uncertainty quantification is carried out with the \textsc{OpenTURNS} library \cite{baudin2017}, reusing the Python automation layer built for the calibration sweeps of \cref{sec:cal-automation}. There, \texttt{itertools.product} is also reused for a probabilistic design.

Besides, extra steps is required for the automated stochastic analyses:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the distribution(\cref{tab:random-variables}, of the analysis as an \textsc{OpenTURNS} \texttt{ComposedDistribution}.
  \item Generate an LHS design of $N$ samples (\cref{sec:lhs}).
  \item Python script for post-process and ploting: correlation, Sobol indices, response PDF.
\end{enumerate}
 
\subsection{Latin Hypercube Sampling} \label{sec:lhs}
 
Monte Carlo (\cref{sec:monte-carlo}) converges only as $1/\sqrt{N}$ and needs many runs of an expensive THM model. Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) is used instead as a variance-reduced alternative \cite{mckay1979,baudin2017}: each marginal is split into $N$ equiprobable strata with one sample per stratum, so the input space is covered evenly and the output statistics stabilise with markedly fewer Cast3M evaluations. For the small number of variables here, the Gaussian-quadrature scheme of \cref{sec:quadrature} offers an even cheaper deterministic cross-check, used in \cref{sec:spatial-effect}.

\section{Sensitivity analysis} \label{sec:sensitivity}
 
\subsection{Analysis A --- permeability \texorpdfstring{$K_0$}{K0} (\texorpdfstring{$A_\Gamma$}{A_Gamma} constant)} \label{sec:sens-K0}
 
In Analysis~A only $K_0$ is random; all other inputs, including the permeability--hydration coefficient $A_\Gamma$ (\texttt{AK}), are held at their calibrated values. Fixing $A_\Gamma$ is deliberate: as shown in \cref{sec:cal-K0-AK}, $K_0$ controls the \emph{magnitude} of the peak whereas $A_\Gamma$ mainly shifts its \emph{timing}, and the two must be balanced to keep the Newton--Raphson solver stable. Keeping $A_\Gamma$ constant isolates the magnitude-driving uncertainty and avoids the divergence reported in \cref{chapter4}. 

With a single random variable the analysis simply evaluates how the gas pressure $p_g^{10}$ respond to $K_0$ over its lognormal distribution (mean $K_0^{\mathrm{cal}}=8.5\times10^{-20}~\si{\square\meter}$, $\mathrm{CoV}=0.30$); the relationship is strongly negative: lower permeability traps more vapour, which results in a higher pressure peak. A Sobol decomposition is not meaningful here, as a single variable trivially accounts for 100\% of the variance $S_1=S_{T}=1$.

Therefore, for Analysis~A, \textsc{OpenTURNS} focuses two main sensitivity outputs (\cref{fig:sensA-corr,fig:sensA-ci}): the input--output correlation, and the bootstrap confidence-interval bands of the indices.

\section{Effect of spatial heterogeneity on structural safety} \label{sec:spatial-effect}
 
The analyses above treat the permeability as a single random variable, constant over the specimen in a given realisation. Real concrete varies from point to point, and local weak zones --- not average properties --- govern failure. The intrinsic permeability $K_{int}$, the most spatially variable and most influential property (\cref{sec:sens-K0}), is therefore additionally described as a \emph{random field} and compared with the homogeneous description. The same lognormal marginal as in Analysis~A is used (mean $=K_0^{\mathrm{cal}}=8.5\times10^{-20}~\si{\square\meter}$, $\mathrm{CoV}=0.30$, calibrated in \cref{chapter4}).
 
\subsection{A simplified isothermal model} \label{sec:spatial-model}
 
Propagating a random field through the full non-linear THC model is impractical for this comparison. The thermal and hydric properties evolve with temperature and hydration, so a parametric system matrix with a \emph{fixed} structure --- as required by the Gaussian-quadrature framework (\texttt{QUADRATU}/\texttt{PARASTAT}, \cref{sec:quadrature}) --- cannot be assembled, and each non-linear realisation is very expensive. To obtain a controlled and tractable comparison, the earlier \emph{simplified TH model} is used instead, in an isothermal setting: the temperature is held constant at room temperature ($T=T_0$), which freezes the constitutive evolution.
 
In this simplified model the permeability is treated in the classical way and is \emph{not} coupled to the effective hydration degree: $K_0$ (\texttt{KINT}) is a plain intrinsic permeability and the coefficient $A_\Gamma$ of the THC formulation is not used. The quantity entering the assembled flow matrix is the liquid-phase effective permeability, function of the capillary pressure $P_c$ only,
\begin{equation}
  K_{\mathrm{eff}}(P_c) \;=\; \frac{k_{rl}\,\rho_l\,K_{int}}{\mu_l},
  \label{eq:keff}
\end{equation}
where $k_{rl}$ is the liquid relative permeability, $\rho_l$ the liquid density and $\mu_l$ the dynamic viscosity. The spatially variable input is $K_{int}$, and the outputs are the pore pressure $p_g$ and the maximum displacement $u_{\max}$ obtained through the poromechanical coupling (\cref{appendixA}). This sacrifices the thermal coupling and the hydration dependence of permeability, but isolates the effect of $K_{int}$ heterogeneity on the hydro-mechanical response in a setting where all three stochastic methods can be applied consistently.
 
\subsection{Methods compared} \label{sec:spatial-methods}
 
The same $K_{int}$ marginal is propagated in three ways, all detailed in \cref{appendixB}:
 
\begin{description}
  \item[OpenTURNS + LHS (random variable).] $K_{int}$ uniform over the specimen, one value per realisation; $N$ Latin-Hypercube samples (\cref{sec:lhs}). This is the homogeneous baseline.
  \item[Cast3M \texttt{ALEA} (random field).] $K_{int}(\mathbf{x})$ generated on the mesh by the Turning Bands Method and the lognormal transformation (\cref{sec:tbm,sec:lognormal-transformation}) with correlation length $L_c$. Given the cost, $10$ realisations are generated and the output statistics are averaged over them --- a small Monte Carlo ensemble (\cref{sec:thm-integration-mc}).
  \item[Gaussian quadrature (random variable).] The deterministic few-point \texttt{QUADRATU}/\texttt{PARASTAT} scheme (\cref{sec:quadrature,sec:quad-cost}), used as a cheap cross-check of the LHS moments.
\end{description}

\begin{draftblock}
\textbf{Analysis.} Two cross-checks and one physical result are expected. First, LHS and quadrature --- both homogeneous random-variable methods --- should return the same mean and standard deviation of $p_g$ and $u_{\max}$, validating the inexpensive quadrature scheme against the sampling estimate. Second, the random-field (\texttt{ALEA}) description should give a \emph{more critical} response: a local low-permeability zone traps the pressure and concentrates the deformation, producing a higher $u_{\max}$ and a wider tail than the homogeneous case, because the weak zone is not compensated by the surrounding material. The $10$-realisation average is a pragmatic estimate given the cost of each run; its scatter indicates the residual Monte Carlo uncertainty. The gap between the homogeneous and heterogeneous results quantifies how much the random-variable assumption underestimates the risk. The full Turning Bands / \texttt{ALEA} construction, the quadrature operators and the Monte Carlo convergence are collected in \cref{appendixB}.
\end{draftblock}
