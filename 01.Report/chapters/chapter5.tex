\section{Sources of uncertainty and concrete heterogeneity}

\subsection{Motivation for probabilistic assessment}

In civil engineering, a structure is considered safe when it remains fit for its intended use throughout its service life \cite{baroth2011}. Deterministic models have traditionally been used to evaluate structural safety. However, the gap between a real physical system and a simplified numerical model, along with the unforeseeable nature of future loads and environmental conditions, inherently leads to uncertainty \cite{baroth2023}.

To manage these risks, modern engineering codes such as the Eurocodes adopt a probabilistic format \cite{baroth2011}. This allows the safety of a structure to be explicitly quantified through its probability of failure $P_f$ (\cref{sec:pf-beta}) and its reliability $R = 1 - P_f$. By introducing random variables into mechanical models and propagating them through structural analyses (\cref{sec:stochastic-framework}), objective decisions can be made based on both the probability of an event and the severity of its consequences \cite{baroth2023}.

\subsection{Classification of uncertainties}

According to reliability theory, uncertainties can be classified into two categories \cite{baroth2011,baroth2023}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aleatoric uncertainties} arise from the natural randomness of the environment and the spatial or temporal variability of material properties. They cannot be reduced; they can only be quantified and accounted for in the design process \cite{baroth2023}.

    \item \textbf{Epistemic uncertainties} stem from incomplete knowledge or insufficient data. They include \emph{model uncertainties} (discrepancy between mathematical models and actual physical processes) and \emph{statistical uncertainties} (limited or inaccurate data). Unlike aleatoric uncertainties, they can be reduced by improving models and gathering more data \cite{baroth2023}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concrete heterogeneity and material variability}

A critical source of aleatoric uncertainty is the inherent heterogeneity of concrete \cite{baroth2023,delarrard2010}. Its mechanical properties depend on the formation history, casting conditions, internal chemical reactions, and long-term degradation processes \cite{delarrard2010}.

This variability manifests as \emph{continuous} spatial fluctuations (e.g.\ Young's modulus, permeability, porosity) and \emph{discrete} defects (e.g.\ micro-cracks, capillary networks) \cite{baroth2023}. Consequently, material parameters must be modelled as random variables or random fields rather than deterministic values (\cref{sec:stochastic-framework}).

\section{Stochastic finite element framework} \label{sec:stochastic-framework}
In structural engineering, material properties such as the Young's modulus, permeability, or thermal conductivity are never perfectly known. As noted by \cite{delarrard2010}, even under strict quality control the compressive strength of high-performance concrete can range from 62 to~100~MPa. Two approaches exist to handle this uncertainty:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \emph{deterministic} analysis assigns a single fixed value to each parameter and produces a unique structural response; it cannot, by itself, quantify the likelihood that a design limit will be exceeded.
    \item A \emph{stochastic} analysis, on the other hand, treats uncertain parameters as random quantities, propagates this uncertainty through the model, and delivers statistical information---mean, variance, probability of failure---about the structural response.
\end{enumerate}

This work adopts the stochastic approach with two levels of description:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Random variable} (\cref{sec:random-variables}):
          each uncertain property is described by a single distribution (mean, standard deviation) applied uniformly over the entire domain. Multiple random variables can be treated simultaneously.

    \item \textbf{Random field} (\cref{sec:random-fields}):
          the property varies from point to point, subject to a spatial correlation structure characterised by a correlation length $L_c$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Uncertain material parameters} \label{sec:uncertain-params}
 
\subsection{Choice of random variables}
 
The parametric study of \cref{chapter4} identified the properties that actually drive the THM response. Five uncertain inputs are retained here, chosen because they govern the heat- and mass-transfer behaviour and because they are the least well known experimentally:
 
\begin{description}
  \item[Intrinsic permeability $K_0$.] Primary control on the magnitude of the gas-pressure peak; reported coefficients of variation are large \cite{delarrard2010}.
  \item[Water-retention parameter $a$.] The van~Genuchten capillary parameter of the sorption isotherm $p_c = a\,(S_l^{-b}-1)^{1-1/b}$; it sets how much water is held and released, and hence the source feeding the pressure build-up.
  \item[Thermal conductivity $\lambda$.] Governs the speed of the thermal front and therefore the timing of drying and dehydration.
  \item[Volumetric heat capacity $\rho C_p$.] Sets the thermal inertia; together with $\lambda$ it fixes the thermal diffusivity $\alpha=\lambda/\rho C_p$.
  \item[Mix-composition factor $\theta_{\mathrm{mix}}$.] A dimensionless multiplier introduced to represent the \emph{imprecision of the mix design} (water-to-cement ratio, cement and aggregate dosage). Because composition simultaneously shifts porosity, permeability and saturation, $\theta_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is not independent of the four parameters above; it is the physical origin of the input correlations discussed in \cref{sec:correlation-analysis}.
\end{description}
 
\subsection{Statistical description}
 
Each strictly positive transport or thermal property is described by a \emph{lognormal} distribution, which guarantees $X>0$ and is the standard choice for such quantities \cite{delarrard2010,sudret2000}; the full definition, moments and coefficient of variation (CoV) are given in \cref{sec:random-variables}. The mean of each variable is set to its value calibrated in \cref{chapter4}, so that the stochastic study is centred on the validated deterministic point. The CoVs are taken from the ranges reported in the literature for concrete \cite{delarrard2010,baroth2023}. \Cref{tab:random-variables} summarises the inputs.

Throughout this chapter the scalar output of interest (QoI) is the gas pressure at the $10~\mathrm{mm}$ probe, $p_{g}^{10}$, i.e.\ the same near-surface gauge used as a calibration target in \cref{chapter4}. \section{Uncertainty propagation with \texorpdfstring{\textsc{OpenTURNS}}{OpenTURNS}} \label{sec:propagation}
 
\subsection{Coupling \textsc{OpenTURNS} to the Cast3M solver}
 
Uncertainty quantification is carried out with the \textsc{OpenTURNS} library \cite{baudin2017}, reusing the Python automation layer already built for the calibration sweeps of \cref{chapter4}. The deterministic THM solver is wrapped as a black-box function $g$: \textsc{OpenTURNS} draws a sample of the input vector $\mathbf{X}=(K_0,a,\lambda,\rho C_p,\theta_{\mathrm{mix}})$, the Python driver writes the corresponding material data, launches the Cast3M \texttt{PASAPAS} run, extracts the $10~\mathrm{mm}$ pressure history and returns the QoI $p_g^{10}=g(\mathbf{X})$ to \textsc{OpenTURNS}. This is exactly the orchestration used for calibration --- the only change is that the parameter values now come from a probabilistic design of experiments instead of a manual sweep. The propagation loop proceeds as follows:
 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the marginal distributions (\cref{tab:random-variables}) and the input correlation (\cref{sec:sensitivity}) as an \textsc{OpenTURNS} \texttt{Distribution}.
  \item Generate an LHS design of $N$ samples $\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\}$ (\cref{sec:lhs}).
  \item \textbf{for} $i=1,\dots,N$: write material data $\rightarrow$ run Cast3M \texttt{PASAPAS} $\rightarrow$ read $p_g^{10}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})$.
  \item Assemble the response sample $\{p_g^{10,(i)}\}$ and post-process: correlation, Sobol indices, response PDF/CDF, $P_f$ and $\beta$.
\end{enumerate}
 
\subsection{Latin Hypercube Sampling} \label{sec:lhs}
 
Crude Monte Carlo sampling (\cref{sec:monte-carlo}) converges only as $1/\sqrt{N}$ and therefore needs many runs of an expensive THM model. Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) is used instead as a variance-reduced alternative \cite{mckay1979,baudin2017}: each marginal is partitioned into $N$ equiprobable strata and exactly one sample is drawn per stratum, so the input space is covered evenly and the estimators of the output mean and variance have a lower variance than crude Monte Carlo at the same $N$. LHS thus reaches stable statistics with markedly fewer Cast3M evaluations, which is decisive given the cost of each run. The Gaussian-quadrature scheme of \cref{sec:quadrature} provides an even cheaper, deterministic alternative for a small number of variables and is used as a cross-check in \cref{sec:spatial-effect}.

\section{Sensitivity analysis} \label{sec:sensitivity}

The THM model involves numerous material parameters. Following \cite{liu2001}, 18 key parameters are selected for sensitivity analysis based on their expected influence on the structural response.

Two complementary measures are extracted from the LHS sample; both are defined in \cref{appendixB}. The \emph{correlation analysis} (Pearson and Spearman rank, \cref{sec:sensitivity-indicators}) gives a cheap first ranking and the sign of each effect, and also displays the input--input correlation imposed by the mix factor $\theta_{\mathrm{mix}}$ (Nataf copula, \cref{sec:correlation}). The \emph{Sobol indices} (\cref{sec:sobol}, \cref{eq:sobol-indices,eq:sobol-total}), computed with \textsc{OpenTURNS}, then give the variance-based decomposition: first-order $S_i$ (main effect), second-order $S_{ij}$ (pairwise interaction) and total-order $S_{T_i}$.

\textbf{Analysis.} Consistent with the deterministic study (\cref{chapter4}), $K_0$ is expected to dominate the variance of $p_g^{10}$ (large, negative correlation: lower permeability traps vapour), followed by $a$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{mix}}$, while $\lambda$ and $\rho C_p$ --- which govern the temperature field --- contribute little to the pressure. A gap between $S_{T_i}$ and $S_i$ for $K_0$, together with sizeable $S_{ij}$ for the pairs $(K_0,a)$ and $(K_0,\theta_{\mathrm{mix}})$, would confirm that water availability and its evacuation act jointly. Any variable with a negligible total index can then be fixed at its mean, reducing the dimensionality of the reliability problem (\cref{sec:reliability}).
\end{draftblock}

\section{Effect of spatial heterogeneity on structural safety} \label{sec:spatial-effect}

The analysis above treats each property as a single \emph{random variable}, constant over the specimen in a given realisation. Real concrete varies from point to point, and local weak zones --- not average properties --- trigger spalling. Two descriptions are therefore compared, both detailed in \cref{appendixB}: a \emph{random variable} (homogeneous per realisation), propagated by LHS or, as a cheap deterministic cross-check, by Gaussian quadrature (\cref{sec:quadrature,sec:quad-cost}); and a \emph{random field} (correlation length $L_c$), generated on the Cast3M mesh by the \texttt{ALEA} operator --- Turning Bands plus lognormal transform (\cref{sec:tbm,sec:lognormal-transformation}) --- and propagated by Monte Carlo through the loop of \cref{sec:thm-integration-mc}.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  % \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figs/ch5_rv_vs_rf.pdf}
  \caption{CDF of $p_g^{10}$ under the homogeneous (random variable) and heterogeneous (random field, \texttt{ALEA}) descriptions, with the resulting $P_f$ / $\beta$.}
  \label{fig:rv-vs-rf}
\end{figure}
 
\begin{draftblock}
\textbf{Analysis.} Spatial heterogeneity is generally \emph{more critical} than the homogeneous assumption: with a random variable the whole structure shares one value per realisation, so weak zones are compensated by strong ones, whereas with a random field local weak zones can initiate failure without compensation. For the same nominal distributions, the \texttt{ALEA}/Monte Carlo case therefore tends to give a higher $P_f$ (lower $\beta$) than the random-variable (quadrature/LHS) case \cite{delarrard2010,fenton2014}; the gap in \cref{fig:rv-vs-rf} quantifies how much the homogeneous assumption underestimates the spalling risk for $p_g^{10}$. The full derivations, the \texttt{ALEA}/Turning-Bands construction and the quadrature operators are collected in \cref{appendixB}.
\end{draftblock}

\begin{draftblock}

The quadrature method models each uncertain parameter as a single random variable, constant over the entire structure (\cref{sec:quadrature}). The Monte Carlo framework, on the other hand, models parameters as random fields discretised via the Turning Band Method, allowing spatial variability within each realisation (\cref{sec:monte-carlo}).

\end{draftblock}

The key consequence for reliability is that spatial heterogeneity generally leads to \emph{more critical} (conservative) results compared to the homogeneous assumption:

\begin{itemize}
    \item With random variables (quadrature), the entire structure has the same material property in each realisation --- weak zones are compensated by strong zones.
    \item With random fields (Monte Carlo), local weak zones can form where failure initiates, without being compensated by surrounding material.
\end{itemize}

This explains why Monte Carlo simulations with spatial variability typically yield higher probabilities of failure (lower $\beta$) than the quadrature approach for the same nominal input distributions \cite{delarrard2010, fenton2014}.
